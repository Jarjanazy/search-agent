# Syrian Olive Oil: A Comprehensive Report

## Summary

Syrian olive oil represents one of the oldest agricultural traditions in the Middle East, with deep cultural, economic, and historical significance. Syria has been a major olive oil producer, cultivating approximately 700,000 hectares with nearly 70 million olive trees. However, the sector currently faces significant challenges including climate change, the ongoing conflict, production decline, quality issues, and water scarcity concerns. Despite these obstacles, Syrian olive oil continues to be recognized for its unique flavors and diverse varieties, reflecting centuries of agricultural expertise in the Mediterranean region.

## Key Findings

### Historical and Cultural Significance
- **Ancient Legacy**: Olive cultivation in Syria dates back over 6,000 years, with Syria and Iran considered the two original centers of olive tree cultivation before spreading throughout the Mediterranean basin
- **Symbolic Importance**: Olive trees are deeply embedded in Syrian culture as symbols of peace, wisdom, and luck, with representation in ancient artifacts and mythology
- **National Pride**: Olive production holds deep cultural and agricultural importance for Syria, serving as a testament to the nation's resilience and ancient heritage

### Production Scale and Economic Importance
- **Agricultural Land**: Approximately 700,000 hectares are currently under olive cultivation
- **Tree Count**: Syria has roughly 69 million olive trees, with approximately 61 million being fruit-bearing
- **Employment**: Over 300,000 farming families depend directly on olive cultivation, with approximately 1 million people depending on olive income either directly or indirectly
- **Global Ranking**: In 2021, Syria ranked seventh in the world for olive oil production, after Portugal, Greece, Italy, and Tunisia

### Production Regions and Varieties
- **Major Production Areas**:
  - **Northern Syria**: Contains 56% of olive tree cultivation, divided between Aleppo and Idlib districts
  - **Central Region**: 18.3% in Hama and Homs districts
  - **Other regions**: Damascus countryside, coastal areas (Latakia, Tartous)
  - **Highest Production**: Hama governorate produced approximately 91,000 tons of olives in 2024

- **Olive Varieties**: Main Syrian cultivars include Kaissy, Zaity (Aleppo area), Dan, Hemplasi, and Souri (Damascus area), and Insassy, Karamani, and Sorani (other regions)
- **Climate-Resistant Varieties**: Syrian olive cultivars are particularly resistant to harsh climate conditions and produce oils with special, highly valued flavors

### Oil Characteristics and Quality
- **Flavor Profile**: Syrian olive oil is renowned for exceptional and diverse flavors that reflect the richness and variety of soil and climate across production regions
- **Production Method**: Produced by pressing olives without chemical additives, ensuring a natural product free from harmful substances
- **Quality Standards**: As of October 2024, Syrian olive oil still falls short of international standards set by the World Standards Authority and International Olive Council
- **Current Quality Issues**: Production quality has become a growing concern, with officials acknowledging the need for improvements to meet international benchmarks

### Recent Production Data
- **2024 Production Outlook**: Expected to yield approximately 55,000 tons of olive oil from about 344,000 tons of olives allocated for pressing (2024 season)
- **2026 Projections**: Ministry of Agriculture anticipates an average to low olive season, expecting at most 65,000 tons of oil from 412,000 tons of olives
- **Significant Decline**: Olive and olive oil production has significantly declined in 2024 compared to the previous year
- **Historical Growth**: Since 1966, olive oil supply has grown at approximately 1% per year

### Production Uses
- **Table Olives**: Approximately 85,000 tons of production (2024) allocated for manufacturing green table olives
- **Oil Production**: Majority of production (~344,000 tons) directed toward pressing for olive oil

### Environmental and Water Concerns
- **Water Scarcity**: Olive oil exports exacerbate Syria's acute water crisis, with thousands of cubic meters of groundwater flowing into global markets through olive oil
- **Resource Depletion**: This water use represents a significant portion of Syria's agricultural water consumption during a critical water shortage period
- **Drought Impact**: Climate change, drought, and flooding in northwest Syria contribute to production challenges and quality decline

### Challenges and Current Status
- **Climate Change**: Negative impacts on production and fruit quality
- **War and Instability**: Ongoing conflict affects agricultural productivity; approximately 40% of olive trees are located outside government-controlled areas in rural Aleppo and Idlib
- **Quality Standards Gap**: Syrian olive oil falls short of International Olive Council standards
- **Per Capita Consumption Decline**: Domestic per capita consumption of olive oil in Syria has dropped significantly
- **Economic Constraints**: High retail prices due to production challenges strain the Syrian economy, putting olive oil increasingly out of reach for many families
- **Seasonal Variability**: Production subject to natural cycles of the olive tree and climate fluctuations

### Government and International Support
- **FAO Partnership**: The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) launched "One Country One Priority Product" initiative, designating olives as Syria's priority product
- **Modernization Efforts**: Government focusing on modern practices, quality control mechanisms, and climate-smart approaches
- **International Collaboration**: Work with Italy and other international partners to improve competitiveness in global markets
- **Strategic Goals**: Aims to modernize the sector, support farming families, and open new trade opportunities for rural development

### Export Policies
- **Export Permissions**: The Syrian government periodically allows olive oil exports to support national income
- **Export Variations**: Export policies fluctuate based on harvest forecasts and domestic consumption needs
- **Trade History**: Previous trade agreements included olive oil exports to Iran in exchange for potatoes and fertilizer (2012)
